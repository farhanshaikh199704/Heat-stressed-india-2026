Heat-Stressed India: The Seen and the Unseen

By Farhan Shaikh  ·  May 2026

Meena is a young 19-year-old migrant worker who accompanies her partner during his journey from the Bundelkhand region of India to the national capital, Delhi. As seasonal migrants, their livelihood comprises agriculture during the pre-monsoon months, followed by contract work from their employer to perform unskilled and semi-skilled labour at a construction site in the metropolitan city. In between rural-to-urban migration, the couple has been expecting their first child. Meena is already in her second trimester but has not yet registered for antenatal care at her village due to her frequent travel.

However, 2026 witnessed Indian cities not only touching record high ambient temperatures, but locations like Delhi having a shift from dry heat to more humid, day-night heat that leaves the body with little to no time to recover. Why does this matter? Because consecutive sweltering days with relatively high humidity levels during pregnancy have been found to be associated with a 1% increase in low birthweight odds. And while it may sound modest, this exposure in a country like India can run to 7–13 days during peak summer. Unfortunately, the effects, as we have understood them so far, get compounded across the entire pregnancy cycle.

The heatwave phenomenon in India is a silent disaster, a crisis that became visible to the world while remaining invisible to those on the margins. While the epidemiology has established that exposure to extreme heat events poses a significant threat to human health, growing evidence now suggests that this prolonged form of stress is already shaping the lives of unborn and born, determining their physiology and how well they may survive the first year on a drastically warming Earth. These larger public health challenges call for urgent policy attention, and decisions grounded in identifying the hotspot regions at risk, the nature of consequences on mothers and their children, and the temporal distribution of stress over the three trimesters of pregnancy.

Limited studies in the past have explored the relationship between heat stress and pregnancy outcomes in India (Kakkad et al 2014, Rekha et al 2024). A persistent struggle in gathering enough adequate evidence lies in the difficulty in attributing exposure at the population scale due to lack of systematic exposure data and lack of a standardised framework to integrate it with health data.

This is the gap that two independent research teams — working separately, with different data, different methods, and different questions — have now begun to close. The Council on Energy, Environment and Water built a composite Heat Risk Index for all 734 Indian districts, using 40 years of climate reanalysis data and 35 indicators spanning hazard, exposure, and vulnerability. Vincent et al. linked the same NFHS-5 health survey data to high-resolution heat exposure records, tracking consecutive sweltering days through each trimester of 209,266 pregnancies across India between 2015 and 2020.

A major finding from these recent studies is that over 70% of Indian districts experienced five or more additional warm nights per summer in 2021-22 compared to the previous decade. This particular climatic change is more severe for maternal health than the increase in hot days, because nighttime is when the body recovers, and a pregnant woman’s already-elevated core temperatures due to the requirements of the fetal-placental unit cannot come down. CEEW additionally found that Delhi experienced 9% higher relative humidity and 6 additional warm nights per summer. Mumbai, on the other hand, recorded 15 additional warm nights. Apparently, the pregnant women in India are no less safe in these metropolitan cities when compared to a location like rural Bihar.

Read together, the two studies form a closed argument. CEEW identifies where the heat stress is worst and what form it takes. Vincent et al. establish what that heat stress does to pregnancies — and specifically which trimester matters most and why.

The trimester that the heat season owns

There is a clear temporal link between the extent of heat stress and the stage of pregnancy as per Vincent et al.. The first trimester is the critical exposure window for preterm birth — each additional consecutive sweltering day raises the odds by 3.6% (aOR 1.036, 95% CI: 1.009–1.064). The second trimester is critical for low birthweight — each additional day raises odds by 1% (aOR 1.010, 95% CI: 1.002–1.018). These are modest per-day increments, but they accumulate across sustained heat events of the kind CEEW documents as increasingly common.

The interaction with India's conception calendar is structural rather than coincidental. Post-monsoon months — October through January — are peak conception periods across northern India. A December conception places the second trimester, the low birthweight window, almost entirely inside March through May. A March conception shifts the first-trimester preterm risk into the same months. Either way, the heat season and the most vulnerable trimester overlap by design. This matters for health policy—it means the risk begins for most women in rural India before they have even registered their pregnancy with the institution, which typically happens at the end of the trimester if at all. The intervention window in real sense is invisible to the system.

The districts where everything converges

CEEW's composite risk index shows that 57% of Indian districts — home to 76% of the country's population — are currently at high to very high heat risk. But the index does more than rank districts by temperature. It weights vulnerability: the share of outdoor agricultural workers, the prevalence of anaemia and diabetes, the proportion of elderly and young children, access to healthcare, electricity, and clean water. Districts with high heat hazard but strong adaptive capacity score differently from those where the hazard lands on populations with no buffer. At the state level, Andhra Pradesh ranks the highest among Indian states with 700 to 1,000 heatwaves days between 1969 and 2019, each associated with at least one fatality

Following the IPCC's AR5 Framework, The districts where both converge — high composite heat risk and poor maternal and child health indicators — are the story's accountability finding. Nandurbar in Maharashtra scores 0.83 on the HRI, has a child underweight prevalence of 57.2%, and only 51% of mothers received a first-trimester antenatal check-up. Bharuch in Gujarat scores 0.92 on the HRI with 45.5% underweight and only 63% first-trimester antenatal coverage. These are the districts where the heat is most sustained, the pregnancies most vulnerable, and the clinical contact least likely to happen during the trimester that matters most.

Policy Action and Way Forward

India's primary institutional response to heat is the Heat Action Plan, which emphasises upon strong local government leadership, training of health professionals, and dissemination of heat alerts. More than 100 HAPs exist across states and districts. A 2023 review found that 95% of them contain no vulnerability assessment and most of them define heatwaves using the India Meteorological Department's official criterion — 40°C for plains, 37°C for coasts. Thus, alerts are issued when that threshold is crossed. The alert system is calibrated to daytime maximum temperatures. The atmospheric phenomenon CEEW documents as rising fastest — sustained humid nights — is the thing the system measures least.

Vincent et al.'s exposure metric, the India Heat Index, is a composite of air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, and solar radiation. CEEW's hazard data comes from 40 years of 12-km resolution reanalysis incorporating both daytime and nighttime temperature extremes. Neither the IHI nor percentile-based nighttime thresholds are currently embedded in India's standard heatwave early warning system, though the IMD has begun incorporating them experimentally.

Most importantly, states where more than 50% of districts fall in the high or very high HRI category should access an additional 10% of State Disaster Response Funds by formally notifying heatwaves as a state-specific disaster. Maharashtra, Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, Kerala, and Telangana all meet this threshold. Those that have not yet notified — including Gujarat — should do so immediately. The additional funds can cover ex gratia payments, agricultural compensation, and critically, capacity building for frontline health workers on heat-maternal health linkages.

India is no stranger to perennial public health concerns that are strongly linked to climate. On average, pollution kills approximately 1.5 to 2 million people in India every year. Even as political regimes change and international agencies declare these problems national emergencies, policies in practice barely move the needle. However, despite the past hinting at nothing but pessimism, it is also true that the public sector of this same country has demonstrated exceptional responses to disasters when they are dealt with in mission mode. It is high time we take notice of the heat problem in a similar fashion, or else the burns may engulf more than we think. Even the angst of the common man.
